What is Bitcoin DCA?

Three questions I get asked all the time: what is Bitcoin, what is DCA, and what happens when you put the two together. Here are my answers, in plain words, with my own trades as the example.

8 Nov 2026 · 7 min read
The short answer

· Bitcoin is digital money with a fixed supply of 21 million coins, run by a public blockchain network instead of a bank.
· DCA (dollar cost averaging) means buying a set amount on a regular schedule, whatever the price.
· Bitcoin DCA is simply the two together: you buy a little Bitcoin, again and again, instead of trying to guess the perfect day.

1. What is Bitcoin?

Bitcoin is money that no company, bank or government runs. It lives on a public network, and anyone can join.

It started with a nine page paper published on 31 October 2008 by someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto. Nobody knows for sure who that is, even today. The network went live on 3 January 2009, in the middle of the financial crisis, and it has been running ever since.

A few things make Bitcoin different from the money in your bank account:

  • Fixed supply. There will only ever be 21 million bitcoin. No one can print more, no matter how much they want to.
  • New supply gets cut in half every four years or so. This is called the halving. The last one was in April 2024, and the next one is expected around 2028.
  • Public record. Every transaction is written to a shared ledger called the blockchain. Anyone can check it, nobody can quietly change it.
  • You can hold it yourself. With a wallet you control, you don't need anyone's permission to hold or send it. That is freedom, and it is also responsibility.

People call it digital gold, a hedge against inflation, a bubble, or a gambling token, depending on who you ask. IMHO, what matters more than the label is this: the price swings hard. Drops of 20 to 30% in a few weeks are normal for Bitcoin, and it has gone through bigger falls than that many times. If that is going to keep you up at night, it is better to know before you buy, not after.

2. What is DCA?

DCA, or dollar cost averaging, means investing the same amount on a regular schedule, no matter what the price is doing.

Say $100 every Monday, or $500 on the first of every month. When the price is low, your money buys more. When the price is high, it buys less. Over time you end up with an average price, instead of betting everything on one day.

It is not a crypto thing. People have been doing it with stocks for decades, and most people already do it without calling it DCA, every time their salary pays into a retirement fund.

A simple example.

$1,000 a month, for four months, while the price moves around:

2$1,000$50,0000.0200
3$1,000$40,0000.0250
4$1,000$50,0000.0200
Total$4,000avg. cost $48,9800.0817

The average of the four prices is $50,000, but the average cost is $48,980. That is because the same $1,000 bought more when the price was down. Not magic, just maths. Prices here are made up to keep it simple.

The real benefit is not the maths though, it is the behaviour. You stop staring at the chart waiting for the perfect entry. A plan makes the decision, not your mood on a red day.

To be fair: DCA doesn't always win. In a market that mostly goes up, putting all your money in on day one usually ends up ahead, because more of it was invested for longer. A Vanguard study on stocks and bonds found lump sum came out ahead about two thirds of the time. DCA is for people who don't know what the market will do next, which, honestly, is all of us.

3. What is Bitcoin DCA?

Bitcoin DCA is buying Bitcoin with dollar cost averaging: a fixed amount, on a fixed schedule, for as long as your plan says.

Out of everything you could buy, I think Bitcoin is the asset where DCA makes the most sense. The price moves so much that guessing the right day is close to impossible, and the regret of guessing wrong is big in both directions. Buy all at once and watch it drop 25% next month. Or wait for the dip, it never comes, and you buy higher. I have been on both sides of that feeling.

With Bitcoin DCA, every drop becomes a cheaper buy instead of a disaster. And it works with any budget. $10 a week or $10,000 a month, same idea.

How to set one up.

  • Pick an amount you can keep paying even in a bad month. If you have to stop because of rent, the amount is too big.
  • Pick a schedule. Weekly, fortnightly, monthly. It doesn't matter much, as long as you stick to it.
  • Pick a platform and check the total cost. Trading fees, spreads, deposit fees. Small buys with a fixed fee can eat a lot of your return. Many exchanges offer recurring buys, compare what they actually charge.
  • Decide where it lives. On the exchange, in a wallet you control, or a mix. Don't move every small buy to a wallet though. Early on I did exactly that, and later found those transfers had cost me about US$1,500 in network fees. Batch them.
  • Decide when you would sell, before you buy. A target, a time frame, or a need. Without an exit plan, DCA is just a way of buying, not a strategy.
  • Write down every buy. Date, amount, price, fee. You will need it for tax, and it keeps you honest.

4. Pure DCA vs. what I actually do.

Pure DCA is fully automatic: same amount, same day, forever. It is simple and it beats most people's attempts at timing the market. But most DCA strategies lack flexibility, which can make a big difference to your overall profit.

So I use DCA as my base and adjust it to my own situation. My rules are simple:

  • Start with DCA. Build the position gradually, instead of betting everything on one entry price.
  • Turn dips into decisions. Keep some cash in reserve, and buy more when the price falls, according to a budget, not emotion.
  • Take profits in stages. Sell a portion when the plan calls for it, and keep the rest exposed to Bitcoin. I also pause buying when prices look overheated.
  • No leverage. Ever. Probably my most important rule. When Bitcoin drops, I can think about whether to buy more, instead of worrying about getting liquidated.

I don't predict the market, because I can't. I follow the plan, and stay flexible.

5. What Bitcoin DCA won't do.

Just as important as what it does:

  • It won't protect you if Bitcoin goes down for years. DCA into something that keeps falling is still a loss.
  • It won't fix high fees. Lots of small buys on an expensive platform can quietly cost you more than you think.
  • It won't keep your coins safe. Custody, backups and recovery are still your job.
  • It won't tell you when to sell. You still need an exit plan.

And the basics still apply. Keep your rent, bills and emergency money separate from your Bitcoin money. Don't borrow to buy. Only invest what you can afford to lose. If you are still deciding whether to buy at all, read Is Bitcoin a good investment right now?

6. Start small. Write everything down.

If you are new to Bitcoin, DCA is IMHO the most forgiving way to start. You learn how buying, holding and sending actually work with small amounts, before the amounts get big. And if after all this you decide Bitcoin is not for you, that is a decision too, and often a good one.

That is why I keep this logbook. Every trade I make is written down, the good ones and the bad ones, so you can see what a DCA plan looks like in real life, not just in theory.

Don't take my word for it. Check every trade yourself: dates, amounts, prices, fees and platforms, including the sells.

It won't make you rich overnight, and it doesn't stop you from losing money. What it does is take the guessing and betting out of buying, which in my experience is where most people get hurt.
